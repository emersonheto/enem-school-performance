\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

\paragraph{Educational inequality and ENEM.}
The relation between school characteristics and ENEM results has been studied mainly with
econometric methods. Greg\'orio Filho et al.~\cite{gregorio2026hiato} apply unconditional
quantile regression and decomposition to ENEM microdata and find that the performance gap
between public and private schools is present along the whole score distribution, grows at the
higher percentiles, and is mostly, but not entirely, explained by the socioeconomic composition
of the students. Their goal is to explain the gap, not to predict school performance, and they
do not evaluate the out-of-sample accuracy of their models.

\paragraph{Machine learning on Brazilian educational data.}
Maia et al.~\cite{maia2021assessing} combine school indicators, socioeconomic variables, and
school-structure variables from the Censo Escolar to model the performance of different Brazilian
school cycles. They report that a non-linear model outperforms a linear one and that the
importance of the variables differs across regions and cycles. Closer to our setting,
Tertulino et al.~\cite{tertulino2026fatores} predict individual ENEM 2022 scores in the state of
Rio Grande do Norte with a random forest and explain it with SHAP \cite{lundberg2017unified}; the
most important predictors are parental education, family income, school type, and digital
access. In the context of higher education, Sakashita et al.~\cite{sakashita2023predicao} apply
machine learning to INEP microdata to predict the ENADE score of computing programs. These
studies use either student-level socioeconomic questionnaires or a single learning algorithm,
and none of them reports cross-validation grouped by geographic unit or nested
cross-validation.

\paragraph{Evaluation methodology.}
When hyperparameters are selected on the same data used to estimate performance, the estimate
is optimistically biased; nested cross-validation avoids this bias \cite{cawley2010overfitting}.
When observations share a spatial or hierarchical structure, as schools in the same municipality
do, random partitions place related observations in both training and test sets, and grouped or
blocked cross-validation gives more realistic estimates \cite{roberts2017crossvalidation}. To
compare several algorithms over the resulting folds, Dem\v{s}ar~\cite{demsar2006statistical}
recommends the Friedman test followed by the Nemenyi post-hoc test.

\paragraph{Positioning of this work.}
Unlike these studies, we work at the school level and use only structural attributes from the
Censo Escolar, without the students' socioeconomic questionnaire. We evaluate six algorithms with
cross-validation grouped by municipality, repeated partitions shared by all algorithms, and nested
hyperparameter tuning, compare them with statistical tests, and interpret the best model with SHAP
and ablations of the administrative network. Since we predict school means rather than individual
scores, our error and $R^2$ values are not directly comparable with student-level studies such
as \cite{tertulino2026fatores}: averaging over students removes much of the individual
variability.
