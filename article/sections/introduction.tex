\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

The Exame Nacional do Ensino M\'edio (ENEM) is the main entrance examination to higher education
in Brazil, and its microdata, published every year by INEP \cite{inep2025enem}, make it possible
to compare the average performance of students across schools. These comparisons consistently
reveal a large gap between public and private schools: using ENEM microdata, Greg\'orio Filho
et al.~\cite{gregorio2026hiato} show that students from public schools score lower than those from
private schools along the whole score distribution, and that most of the gap is explained by
the socioeconomic composition of the student body, such as family income and parental education.

Socioeconomic composition, however, is largely outside the control of a school or of an
education department. What schools and public policy can act upon are the structural
characteristics of a school: its infrastructure, its size, its staff, and the network that
administers it. This raises the question that guides this work: \emph{to what extent do the
structural characteristics of a Brazilian high school predict the average ENEM score of its
students?}

To answer it, we build a school-level data set that joins the ENEM 2025 results with the
Censo Escolar 2024 \cite{inep2024censo}, INEP's annual census of basic-education schools. The
data set contains 23{,}374 schools with at least ten candidates present in all ENEM tests, and
45 structural attributes grouped into infrastructure, size and resources, and administrative
network and location. The target is the school mean of the five ENEM scores. We frame the
problem as regression and compare six algorithms (linear regression, $k$-nearest neighbors,
support vector regression, decision tree, random forest, and gradient boosting) under a
validation protocol designed to avoid optimistic estimates: cross-validation grouped by
municipality, repeated three times with five folds and with identical partitions for every
algorithm, nested cross-validation for hyperparameter tuning, and the Friedman test with the
Nemenyi post-hoc test to compare the algorithms. Finally, we interpret the best model with
SHAP values and with ablations that remove the administrative network, which acts as a proxy
for the socioeconomic profile of the students.

The main contributions of this work are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a reproducible, publicly available pipeline that builds a school-level data set from the
        official ENEM 2025 and Censo Escolar 2024 microdata;
  \item a comparison of six regression algorithms under grouped, repeated, and nested
        cross-validation, with statistical tests over the results;
  \item an analysis of how much of the predictive power comes from the administrative network
        and how much from the remaining structural characteristics, including the
        distribution of the errors across networks and regions.
\end{itemize}

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related-work} reviews related
work. Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the data and the exploratory analysis, and
Section~\ref{sec:methodology} the preprocessing and the evaluation protocol.
Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the results of the spot-checking and of the tuned models, and
Section~\ref{sec:interpretation} interprets the best model. Section~\ref{sec:reproducibility}
describes the materials made available, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.
