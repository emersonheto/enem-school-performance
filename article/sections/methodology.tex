\subsection{Preprocessing}
\label{sec:preprocessing}

The preprocessing follows the issues found in Section~\ref{sec:data}. Two attributes are dropped
because they are redundant: the region (\texttt{CO\_REGIAO}), determined by the state, and the
number of high-school classes (\texttt{QT\_TUR\_MED}), with Spearman 0.96 to enrolments. The
extreme-value code 88888 becomes a missing value, and a binary indicator records that the school
had it, so that the information is not lost. The remaining attributes are treated by type:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Counts} (8): median imputation, $\log(1+x)$ to reduce the skew, and standardization;
  \item \emph{Binary indicators} (31, plus the 2 extreme-value indicators): imputation by the most
        frequent value and no scaling, since they are already 0/1 and scaling would give rare
        indicators a large weight in distance-based models;
  \item \emph{Categorical attributes} (administrative network, location, differentiated location,
        and state): imputation by the most frequent value and one-hot encoding, ignoring categories
        unseen in training.
\end{itemize}
The result has 79 columns. Every step that learns from the data (medians, modes, means, scales,
and categories) is part of a scikit-learn \texttt{Pipeline} together with the model, so it is
fitted only on the training schools of each fold and no information leaks from the test schools.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

\paragraph{Grouped partitions.} Schools of the same municipality share socioeconomic context and
tend to have similar scores. If they were split between training and test, a model could predict a
school from its neighbours and the error would be underestimated \cite{roberts2017crossvalidation}.
All partitions are therefore grouped by municipality (\texttt{GroupKFold}): the 5{,}201
municipalities, and not the schools, are assigned to the folds.

\paragraph{Repetitions.} The outer evaluation uses 5 folds repeated 3 times, each repetition with a
different shuffle of the municipalities (seed $42 + r$ for repetition $r$), for 15 measurements per
algorithm. The folds of each repetition are computed once and shared by all the algorithms, so the
comparisons are paired.

\paragraph{Metrics.} The main metric is the root mean squared error (RMSE), in ENEM points: it is
directly interpretable on the scale of the exam and penalizes large errors, which matter when the
model is used to flag schools far from what their structure predicts. The mean absolute error (MAE)
and $R^2$ are reported as secondary metrics. Two baselines give the reference points: the mean of
the training schools, and a linear regression on the administrative network alone. To measure
how much the network explains, models are evaluated on three feature-set variants: A (all
attributes), B (without the administrative network), and C (without the network and the
school-meal indicator).

\paragraph{Reproducibility.} The code, the school-level table, the per-fold results, and the
notebooks with their outputs are available at
\url{https://github.com/emersonheto/enem-school-performance}. A script downloads the INEP files,
records their SHA-256 hashes, and builds the table; the notebooks check these hashes before
running. The preprocessing, the folds, and the evaluation are implemented once and shared by all
notebooks, library versions are pinned (Python 3.12, scikit-learn 1.9.1), and all random
processes use fixed seeds (42, and $42 + r$ for repetition $r$). On the same platform the metrics
are reproduced exactly; on others, such as Google Colab, small numerical differences may appear
without changing the ranking of the algorithms.

\subsection{Spot-checking and hyperparameter tuning}
\label{sec:tuning}

We first compare six algorithms with different inductive biases, using the default
hyperparameters of scikit-learn: linear regression, $k$-NN, a decision tree, SVR with RBF kernel,
random forest, and gradient boosting. For the SVR, the target is standardized during training
(\texttt{TransformedTargetRegressor}), since its default $C$ and $\varepsilon$ assume a target of
unit scale, while the school mean has a standard deviation of 62 points; predictions are converted
back to ENEM points.

The best algorithms of the spot-checking are then tuned with nested cross-validation
\cite{cawley2010overfitting}. The outer loop is the same $3 \times 5$ grouped protocol, which
measures the error. Inside each outer training set, a grid search (\texttt{GridSearchCV}) with 3
folds, also grouped by municipality, selects the hyperparameters by RMSE; the selected model is
refitted on the whole outer training set and evaluated once on the outer test fold. Grouping the
inner folds matters: otherwise the search would favour models that exploit neighbouring schools,
such as $k$-NN with a small $k$. Table~\ref{tab:grids} lists the grids. They were kept small
because each combination is trained 45 times; for the SVR, $C$ is limited to 10 because $C = 100$
took about 140~s per fit, against about 10~s for $C = 10$.

\subsection{Statistical comparison}
\label{sec:stats}

Following Dem\v{s}ar~\cite{demsar2006statistical}, the algorithms are compared with the Friedman test over
the 15 paired measurements of RMSE: in each of them (a block), the algorithms are ranked from 1
(lowest error) upwards, and the test checks whether the average ranks differ more than expected by
chance ($\alpha = 0.05$). When it rejects the null hypothesis, the Nemenyi post-hoc test declares
two algorithms different if their average ranks differ by at least the critical difference
$CD = q_\alpha \sqrt{k(k+1)/(6N)}$, with $k$ algorithms and $N = 15$ blocks. The mean baseline is
left out of the tests. Since the training sets of different folds and repetitions overlap, the
measurements are not independent and the p-values are optimistic; we use the tests as a guide
together with the size of the differences in RMSE points, not as proof of superiority.
